## Unit 1: First phrases (a1-first-phrases)

- **Location:** "Hello and goodbye" table + bullet on *Buonanotte*; "Typical mistakes" row 2; Summary ("*buonanotte* is only for bedtime")
  - **Severity:** should fix. **Confidence:** high
  - **Problem:** "Buonanotte is only for bedtime … You don't greet anyone with it, even late at night" is too absolute. You can't use *buonanotte* to say hello, but Italians commonly use it to say goodbye late at night, for example leaving a friend's house or a restaurant at midnight, even when nobody is going straight to bed. Treccani defines it as the formula used when taking leave "a ora tarda, prima d'andare a letto". The rule as written would make learners avoid a very normal late-night goodbye. (Mastery m-02 already sidesteps this by leaving *Buonanotte!* out of the options, which suggests the author sensed the problem.)
  - **Fix:** Table row: `| **Buonanotte!** | Good night: a goodbye late at night, or at bedtime | any |`. Bullet: "**Buonanotte** is only a goodbye, never a hello: say it when you part late at night or when someone goes to bed. Arriving somewhere at 10 PM, you still say *buonasera*." Summary: "*buonanotte* is only for saying goodbye late at night."
  - **Source:** Treccani, *buonanotte*.

- **Location:** "Hello and goodbye" table (*Buongiorno*, *Buonasera* listed as "Hello" only), and exercise **a1-first-phrases-p-08**
  - **Severity:** error (p-08), should fix (reading). **Confidence:** high
  - **Problem:** *Buongiorno* and *buonasera* are used when leaving as well as when arriving. Leaving a shop with "Buongiorno!" or "Buonasera!" is extremely common, often instead of *arrivederci*. Treccani's *buonasera* entry says "incontrandosi o anche accomiatandosi" (when meeting, or also when leaving). That makes p-08 ("Which of these can mean both 'hello' and 'goodbye'?") wrong: the distractor *Buongiorno* is also a correct answer. (*Salve* is fine as a distractor because Treccani gives it only for meeting.)
  - **Fix (reading):** Change the *Buongiorno* and *Buonasera* "When" cells to "Hello or goodbye, in the morning and early afternoon" and "Hello or goodbye, from the afternoon/evening". Add to the bullet: "Both also work as a goodbye when you leave a shop or office."
  - **Fix (p-08):** Change the prompt to "Which of these can mean both 'hello' and 'goodbye', but only with friends?" and replace the distractor *Buongiorno* with *Buonanotte*. Or keep the prompt and use distractors *Arrivederci*, *Salve*, *Piacere*.
  - **Source:** Treccani, *buonasera*.

- **Location:** "Hello and goodbye": *Buonasera* "from late afternoon"; bullet "The switch … happens roughly in the late afternoon"; Summary "Greet with *buongiorno* until late afternoon"
  - **Severity:** minor. **Confidence:** medium
  - **Problem:** In much of Italy (Tuscany and the centre-south especially) *buonasera* starts right after lunch, around 13:00–14:00. Treccani's *buonasera* entry says "nel pomeriggio o di sera". "Late afternoon" describes northern usage, not the norm everywhere. The text does say it varies by region, but it anchors the rule too late.
  - **Fix:** "The switch happens sometime in the afternoon: right after lunch in many regions, later in others. If you're unsure in the afternoon, *buonasera* or *salve* is safe."
  - **Source:** Treccani, *buonasera*.

- **Location:** "Please, thank you and sorry": the scusa / mi dispiace split ("three words, and they aren't interchangeable"; *mi dispiace* for "being late"), and exercise **a1-first-phrases-p-14**
  - **Severity:** should fix. **Confidence:** medium-high
  - **Problem:** The real difference isn't small vs big. *Scusa/scusi* (or *scusami/mi scusi*) apologises for something you did. *Mi dispiace* expresses regret or sympathy, including for things that aren't your fault. Apologising for being late is typically *Scusa il ritardo / Scusate il ritardo / Scusa, sono in ritardo*, so presenting lateness as a *mi dispiace* case and calling the words "not interchangeable" misleads learners. In p-14 (declining a friend's party), "Scusa, non posso venire" is natural too, so the distractor *Scusa!* is defensible. *Mi dispiace* is the better answer there, but the exercise isn't clean.
  - **Fix (reading):** "**Scusa / scusi** apologises for something you did, especially small things: getting someone's attention, bumping into someone, interrupting, being late. **Mi dispiace** expresses regret or sympathy: bad news, saying no, not being able to help. It literally means 'it displeases me'." Change the example to `> Mi dispiace, non posso. / I'm sorry, I can't.` or keep the late example as `> Scusa, sono in ritardo. / Sorry, I'm late.` under *scusa*.
  - **Fix (p-14):** Replace the distractor *Scusa!* with *Grazie!*, or change the scenario to something with no fault involved: "A friend tells you their cat is ill. What do you say?" → *Mi dispiace!*

- **Location:** "Formal and informal": "children are taught to use *Lei* with adults they don't know, like a teacher or a friend's parent"
  - **Severity:** minor. **Confidence:** medium
  - **Problem:** In Italian primary schools, children very commonly use *tu* with the *maestra*; *Lei* with teachers typically starts in middle school (*scuola media*). Naming "a teacher" as the example of children using *Lei* is inaccurate for young children.
  - **Fix:** "...while children are taught to use *Lei* with adults they don't know, like a friend's parent or a shopkeeper."

- **Location:** "Formal and informal": "When you're not sure, use the formal version. Being too polite is never a problem." (also used as the hint for **a1-first-phrases-p-10**)
  - **Severity:** minor. **Confidence:** medium
  - **Problem:** "Never a problem" is an absolute claim, and it isn't quite true: insisting on *Lei* with a peer who has used *tu*, or with young people in casual settings, comes across as cold or distancing. The safe-default advice is right; the absolute isn't.
  - **Fix:** "When you're not sure, use the formal version: it's the safer mistake, and the other person will offer *tu* if they want it."

- **Location:** "How are you?" table: "**Sto male.** I'm not well." and exercise **a1-first-phrases-p-19**
  - **Severity:** minor. **Confidence:** medium
  - **Problem:** *Sto male* is fairly strong ("I feel bad / I'm ill"). The everyday mild answer to "how are you?" is *Non sto bene* or *Non tanto bene*. p-19 asks for "I'm not well" and accepts only *sto male*, so the more literal *non sto bene* gets rejected locally (the LLM fallback may rescue it).
  - **Fix:** Gloss as "**Sto male.** I'm not well / I feel bad." In p-19 add `"non sto bene"` to correct_answer.

- **Location:** **a1-first-phrases-p-13** (a friend says *Grazie!*, answer *Prego!*)
  - **Severity:** minor. **Confidence:** medium
  - **Problem:** *Prego* is correct, but between friends *Figurati!* or *Di niente!* is much more typical. Not an error at A1. Consider making the thanker a stranger or shop customer (as m-05 does), or adding a one-line note in the reading: "With friends you'll also hear *Figurati!* or *Di niente!*".

- **Location:** **a1-first-phrases-p-24** ("Tell someone your name is Elena", accepts only `mi chiamo Elena`)
  - **Severity:** minor. **Confidence:** medium
  - **Problem:** *Sono Elena* is an equally natural answer, and the reading's own example translates *Mi chiamo Sara* as "I'm Sara". *Sono* isn't formally taught until the next unit, but a learner who types it is right.
  - **Fix:** Add `"sono Elena"` to correct_answer.

- **Location:** **a1-first-phrases-p-27**
  - **Severity:** minor (data consistency, not language). **Confidence:** high
  - **Problem:** `correct_answer` is a bare string (`"dice"`), while the other fill_blank items in this file use arrays. Check that the validator handles both. (Same pattern in essere p-12–p-16, p-30, m-01–m-03 and numbers p-08, p-22, m-12; presumably fine if the loader normalises.)

Not flagged (checked and fine): the formal/informal table, *Salve* as neutral, *Arrivederci* / *A presto*, *Prego?* from a shop assistant, *Permesso*, *Non c'è male*, *sto bene* not *sono bene*, *Piacere / Piacere mio*, *Come si dice…?*, *Sì / si*, the *buon / buona / buone* wishes, *Auguri / Complimenti / Congratulazioni*, *Diamoci del tu / Possiamo darci del tu?*, and all other exercises.

**Overall (First phrases):** Solid, natural, and mostly accurate on register. The *tu/Lei* section is well judged and up to date, including the asymmetry and how people switch to *tu*. The main weaknesses are three rules stated too absolutely: *buonanotte* "only for bedtime" (it's the normal late-night goodbye), *buongiorno/buonasera* presented as hello-only (they're also goodbyes, which makes p-08 wrong), and *scusa* vs *mi dispiace* framed as small vs big rather than "apology for what you did" vs "regret/sympathy". Fixing those three plus p-08 and p-14 would make the unit reliable.

## Unit 2: Subject pronouns & essere (a1-essere)

- **Location:** **a1-essere-p-26** ("You introduce yourself. Which is the natural, neutral way?" answer *Sono Marco.*, distractor *Io sono Marco.*), and "Dropping the pronoun" ("Saying *Io sono Marco* … sounds emphatic, as if you're insisting on *I*")
  - **Severity:** should fix. **Confidence:** medium-high
  - **Problem:** When introducing yourself, *Io sono Marco* (often followed by *piacere*) is extremely common and natural in speech, especially in a round of introductions. It isn't felt as insisting. Pronoun-drop is the right general rule, but this particular example is one where Italians freely use *io*. Marking *Io sono Marco* as wrong in a multiple-choice question penalises natural Italian.
  - **Fix (reading):** Use a less ritual example, such as "Saying *Io sono di Milano* in a neutral sentence is not wrong, but it usually sounds emphatic…", and add: "(In introductions, *Io sono Marco* is common too.)"
  - **Fix (p-26):** Replace the distractor *Io sono Marco.* with an actual error, e.g. *Mi sono Marco.* (a common learner blend of *mi chiamo* and *sono*), or rewrite the item: "Which sentence stresses *I*, as in 'I'm the one from Rome'?" → *Io sono di Roma.*

- **Location:** **a1-essere-p-38** (hint "Describing yourself with an adjective uses essere."; distractor *Sto stanco.*)
  - **Severity:** minor. **Confidence:** medium
  - **Problem:** As a hint, "describing yourself with an adjective uses essere" is an absolute that the course will contradict later (*stare zitto*, *stare tranquillo*, *stare attento*, which the reading itself mentions). *Sto stanco* is also exactly the central-southern regional usage the reading says is "normal in those regions". It's fine as the wrong option in standard Italian, but the hint should say so.
  - **Fix:** Hint: "In standard Italian, how tired, happy etc. you are uses essere: *sono stanco*. (*Sto stanco* is regional.)"

- **Location:** "Essere: to be" bullet: "Before a vowel, *dove* (where) and *come* (how, what … like) usually drop their final *e*"
  - **Severity:** minor. **Confidence:** medium
  - **Problem:** In writing, elision of *dove/come* is standard essentially before forms of *essere* starting with *e/è* (*dov'è, com'è, dov'erano*). Before other vowels it's normally not written: *Dove abiti?*, *Come andiamo?*. "Usually … before a vowel" will make learners write *Dov'abiti?*.
  - **Fix:** "Before *è*, *dove* (where) and *come* (how, what … like) drop their final *e*: *Dov'è?* (Where is it?), *Com'è?* (What's it like?)."

- **Location:** "Essere: to be": "Profession" (*Sono studente*), and exercises **p-20** / **p-42** / **m-13**
  - **Severity:** minor. **Confidence:** high
  - **Problem:** Not wrong, but a woman says *Sono studentessa*, and many professions have feminine forms. Since the unit stresses that women say *italiana*, a female learner may wrongly generalise *Sono studente* to herself. A short note would prevent that.
  - **Fix:** After the profession examples add: "Many job words have a feminine form: a woman says *Sono studentessa*. The unit on nouns covers these."

- **Location:** **a1-essere-m-08** ("You ask three friends 'Are you tired?'" → *Siete stanchi?*)
  - **Severity:** minor. **Confidence:** medium
  - **Problem:** If the three friends are women, the answer is *Siete stanche?*. Masculine plural for a mixed or unknown group hasn't been taught at this point; the reading only shows *Siamo tedeschi*. Both the prompt and the distractors are otherwise fine.
  - **Fix:** "You ask three friends (two men and a woman)…", or accept `"Siete stanche?"` as well. MC can't accept both, so the gender cue is the simpler fix.

- **Location:** "Tu, Lei and voi": *Lei* "for … a teacher" (and **a1-essere-m-07**, "your teacher, whom you don't know well")
  - **Severity:** minor. **Confidence:** low-medium
  - **Problem:** Consistent with the course. Just note the primary-school caveat from Unit 1 (young children often use *tu* with the *maestra*). m-07 is fine as written because of "whom you don't know well". No change needed unless Unit 1 is changed.

Not flagged (checked and fine): the pronoun table; *egli/ella/essi/esse* advice (matches Crusca and Treccani); "no everyday pronoun for 'it'"; *Lei* = 3rd person with gender agreement to the addressee; the optional capital L; *Loro* as old-fashioned formal plural; southern formal *voi*; all four reasons to keep the pronoun; *Anch'io / anche io*; the *essere* table; *è / e* and *E'* vs *È*; *essere di* + city vs nationality, and *da* only with movement verbs; *Sono insegnante* vs *È un bravo insegnante*; *c'è / ci sono*; the essere/stare split and the regional note; questions by intonation; *non* before the verb; *No, non sono stanco*. All exercises other than those above are correct, unambiguous and appropriately hinted (p-09 and p-17's "X, or Y" answers are well designed).

**Overall (Subject pronouns & essere):** This unit is accurate and careful. The rules are stated with the right hedges, and the regional notes (*stare* for *essere*, formal *voi*) are real and well placed. There are no outright errors. The one item to fix is the treatment of *Io sono Marco* as non-neutral, which is shaky for self-introductions and makes p-26 penalise natural Italian. The others are small accuracy refinements: the *dove/come* elision rule, the p-38 hint, feminine professions, and the gender of "three friends".

## Unit 3: Numbers 0–100 (a1-numbers)

- **Location:** "Numbers with nouns": "**Uno** before a noun becomes **un** or **una**"; "Typical mistakes" (*uno fratello → un fratello*, "Before a noun, *uno* becomes *un* or *una*"); Summary; hints of **a1-numbers-p-21** and **p-29**
  - **Severity:** should fix. **Confidence:** high
  - **Problem:** The rule is stated as absolute but is false: before masculine nouns starting with *s* + consonant, *z*, *gn*, *ps*, *x*, *y*, *uno* stays *uno* (*uno studente*, *uno zaino*), and *una* becomes *un'* before a vowel (*un'amica*). The unit's own example list includes *venti studenti*, so a learner applying the rule would produce *un studente*. The forward reference to the articles unit is good, but the rule must not be absolute.
  - **Fix (reading):** "**Uno** before a noun usually becomes **un** or **una**, like 'a' in English: *un fratello*, *una sorella*. (It stays *uno* before some words, like *uno studente*. The unit on nouns and articles explains when.)" Typical-mistakes "Why": "Before most nouns, *uno* becomes *un* or *una*." p-21 / p-29 hints: "Before most masculine nouns, uno becomes un."

- **Location:** "Numbers 21–100", adjustment 2 ("**Tre at the end takes an accent**") and typical mistake *ventitre*
  - **Severity:** minor. **Confidence:** high
  - **Problem:** The rule is correct, but it doesn't say which accent. Standard spelling is the acute *é* (*ventitré*), and with `strict_accents` on (p-14, m-05, m-10), a learner who types *ventitrè* (very common, even among Italians) needs to know why. If the validator rejects *è*, the reading must say so; if it accepts it, fine.
  - **Fix:** "**Tre at the end takes an accent** (*é*, slanting up to the right), because the stress falls on it: **ventitré**…". Also check how strict_accents treats *è* vs *é* for these items.

- **Location:** "Numbers 0–20": "**11 to 16** end in **-dici** … with the unit first: *un-dici*, *do-dici*, *tre-dici*"
  - **Severity:** minor. **Confidence:** medium
  - **Problem:** True, but *quattordici* and *quindici* don't look like *quattro + dici* / *cinque + dici*, and *quindici* in particular has a hidden unit. p-08's hint "14 = quattro + dici" is fine, but m-03's distractor *cinquedici* is exactly what a learner following "unit first" would build. Mention the irregular stem.
  - **Fix:** Add: "The unit is sometimes disguised: *quattordici* (14), *quindici* (15), *sedici* (16)."

- **Location:** "Numbers 21–100": "most look like their unit: *quattro → quaranta* …"
  - **Severity:** minor. **Confidence:** low
  - **Problem:** *quaranta* is the least transparent example to lead with (and *cinquanta* isn't mentioned). Not wrong. Optionally start the list with *sei → sessanta* and say "*quaranta* (40) and *cinquanta* (50) are less obvious".

- **Location:** **a1-numbers-p-27** (distractor *ventuno anno*)
  - **Severity:** minor. **Confidence:** low
  - **Problem:** Singular after compounds of *uno* (*ventun anno*) exists as an older or literary usage, so *ventuno anno* isn't strictly "never" Italian. It's fine as a wrong answer for a modern A1 learner. No change needed; noting it only in case a native reviewer raises it.

Not flagged (checked and fine): the 0–20 table and spellings (*diciassette, diciotto, diciannove*); the tens; vowel drop before *uno/otto* (*ventuno, ventotto, trentuno, quarantuno, cinquantotto, novantuno*); one word with no *e*; *cento* without *uno*; noun plural from 2; *vent'anni / venti anni*; *ventun anni / ventuno anni*; phone numbers digit by digit or in pairs, and the worked examples; *sei* = six / you are. All exercises' answers are correct, distractors are clearly wrong, and accepted alternatives (*trentun/trentuno anni*, *quarant'anni/quaranta anni*) are right.

**Overall (Numbers 0–100):** Accurate and well organised. All number forms, spellings and elisions are correct, and the age spellings (*vent'anni*, *ventun anni*) are handled with appropriate tolerance. The one real problem is the absolute rule "*uno* before a noun becomes *un* or *una*", which is false before *s* + consonant / *z* and contradicts the unit's own *studenti* example. It needs a "usually" and a pointer to the articles unit. The remaining items are small clarifications (which accent on *-tré*, disguised units in 14–15).
