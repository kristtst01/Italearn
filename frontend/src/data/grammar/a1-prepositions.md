# Prepositions

You've been saying *al bar*, *sul tavolo* and *nella borsa* for a while. Each of those is a small word like *a* (at, to), *su* (on) or *in* (in) joined to an article. This unit explains what's inside them, and the rest of the prepositions you need at A1: where you are and where you're going, how you travel, where you come from, whose something is, and how to say "some".

Prepositions work differently from the grammar so far. Verb endings and articles follow rules you can learn once and apply everywhere. Prepositions mostly don't. Each one covers a spread of uses that don't add up to a single meaning, so none of them translates cleanly into one English word: *a* can be "at", "to" or "in", and English "in" can be *in*, *a* or *nel*. English says *in* Rome and *in* Italy; Italian says *a Roma* but *in Italia*. This is true of prepositions in every language, and they're one of the last things even advanced learners get fully right.

So treat this unit differently. Where there's a pattern, it's given here, but most patterns are rules of thumb with exceptions. The reliable way to learn prepositions is in phrases: learn *in ufficio* and *al bar* as wholes, the way you learn a noun with its article, and the right preposition will come with the phrase.

## The simple prepositions

Italian has eight short prepositions. You already know several. The English in the middle column is only a rough guide to how each one is often translated:

| Preposition | Often translated as | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **di** | of, from (a city), 's | *Sono di Roma.* *Un bicchiere di vino.* |
| **a** | at, to, in (a city) | *Vado a casa.* *Abito a Roma.* |
| **da** | from, at or to someone's place | *Vengo da Londra.* *Vado da Marco.* |
| **in** | in, to (a country, many places) | *Abito in Italia.* *Sono in ufficio.* |
| **con** | with | *Esco con Giulia.* |
| **su** | on | *Il libro è sul tavolo.* |
| **per** | for, in order to | *Questo è per te.* *Studio per lavorare in Italia.* |
| **tra** / **fra** | between, in (time) | *Tra la banca e il bar.* *Fra cinque minuti.* |

*Te* in *per te* is "you" after a preposition: *per te* (for you), *con te* (with you).

> Il caffè è per te.
> The coffee is for you.

**Tra** and **fra** mean exactly the same. People choose by sound, to avoid *tra tre* or *fra fratelli*, but either is correct.

> La farmacia è tra la banca e il bar.
> The pharmacy is between the bank and the bar.

> Arrivo fra cinque minuti.
> I'm arriving in five minutes.

## Prepositions with the article

When **di, a, da, in** and **su** come before *il, lo, la, i, gli, le*, they join with the article into one word. This isn't optional: *a il bar* is always *al bar*.

| | **il** | **lo** | **l'** | **la** | **i** | **gli** | **le** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **di** | del | dello | dell' | della | dei | degli | delle |
| **a** | al | allo | all' | alla | ai | agli | alle |
| **da** | dal | dallo | dall' | dalla | dai | dagli | dalle |
| **in** | nel | nello | nell' | nella | nei | negli | nelle |
| **su** | sul | sullo | sull' | sulla | sui | sugli | sulle |

The pattern is regular once you see it:

- The article keeps its shape: *il* → *-l*, *lo* → *-llo*, *la* → *-lla*, *l'* → *-ll'*, *i* → *-i*, *gli* → *-gli*, *le* → *-lle*. Whatever decides the article (*lo* before z, *l'* before a vowel) decides the joined form too: *nello zaino*, *all'università*.
- **In** changes to **ne-**: *nel*, *nella*, *negli*. **Di** changes to **de-**: *del*, *della*, *degli*.

> Il cellulare è nella borsa. Le chiavi sono sul tavolo.
> The phone is in the bag. The keys are on the table.

**Con** can join too, but only two forms are still in everyday use, **col** (*con il*) and **coi** (*con i*), and both are optional: *col latte* and *con il latte* are both fine. The other joined forms of *con* are old-fashioned. **Per**, **tra** and **fra** don't join in modern Italian: *per il bambino*, *tra le case*.

**Da** itself never loses its vowel before another vowel, so it isn't confused with *di*: *da Anna*, not *d'Anna*. Only the article part shortens: *dall'ufficio*.

This is a big table, but you won't need to look at it for long. Most of the time you'll hear the right form before you think of it, the way you already do with *al bar*.

## Where you are and where you're going

Italian uses the same preposition for being somewhere and going there. *A Roma* means both "in Rome" and "to Rome":

> Abito a Roma. Domani vado a Roma.
> I live in Rome. Tomorrow I'm going to Rome.

So the question is only which preposition goes with which place.

### Cities and countries

Today the usual rule is **a with cities** and **in with countries and regions**:

> Abito a Firenze, in Toscana, in Italia.
> I live in Florence, in Tuscany, in Italy.

> Vado a Parigi, in Francia.
> I'm going to Paris, in France.

With countries, *in* usually has no article, even though the country itself has one (*l'Italia*, *la Francia*). Plural names are the exception: they take the joined form, so *gli Stati Uniti* gives **negli Stati Uniti** (in or to the United States).

Islands vary. Large ones usually take *in*: *in Sicilia* (in Sicily). Small ones often take *a*, like cities: *a Capri*. Learn them as you meet them.

### Places in town

Here there's no single rule. Some places take **a**, some **in**, some take the article and some don't. The best way is to learn each place with its preposition, the way you learn a noun with its article. These are the common ones:

| **a**, no article | **in**, no article | **a** + article | **da** + a person |
|---|---|---|---|
| *a casa* (home, at home) | *in centro* (in town, downtown) | *al bar* | *dal medico* (to or at the doctor's) |
| *a scuola* (at school) | *in ufficio* (at the office) | *al ristorante* | *da Marco* (at Marco's) |
| *a letto* (in bed, to bed) | *in banca* (at the bank) | *al cinema* | *dai genitori* (at your parents') |
| *a teatro* (at the theatre) | *in stazione* (at the station) | *al supermercato* (at the supermarket) | |
| | *in farmacia* (at the pharmacy) | *al mare* (at the seaside) | |
| | *in biblioteca* (at the library) | *all'università* | |
| | *in piazza*, *in cucina*, *in giardino* | | |
| | *in montagna*, *in campagna* | | |

Two patterns help a little:

- Rooms, and many shops and offices, especially ones ending in **-ia**, take *in*: *in cucina*, *in farmacia*, *in pizzeria* (at the pizzeria). This is a tendency, not a rule: *al supermercato* and *al bar* don't follow it.
- When you say *which* one, the article comes back: *in ufficio* (at the office), but *nell'ufficio di Marco* (in Marco's office); *in cucina*, but *nella cucina di Giulia*.

> Stasera sono a casa. Domani lavoro in ufficio, poi vado al supermercato.
> Tonight I'm at home. Tomorrow I'm working at the office, then I'm going to the supermarket.

*Al mare* but *in montagna* and *in campagna* is a classic pair: the seaside takes *a*, the mountains and the countryside take *in*.

### At someone's place: da

To be at, or go to, someone's home or workplace, use **da** with the person. English says "at the doctor's"; Italian just says *dal medico*:

> Oggi vado dal medico. Stasera ceniamo da Giulia.
> Today I'm going to the doctor's. Tonight we're having dinner at Giulia's.

*Da* goes with the **person**, *a* or *in* with the **place**: *dal medico*, but *in ospedale* (at the hospital).

## How you travel

For transport, use **in** with no article:

> Vado a Roma in treno. Giulia va in ufficio in bici.
> I'm going to Rome by train. Giulia goes to the office by bike.

| | |
|---|---|
| *in treno* | by train |
| *in macchina* | by car |
| *in aereo* | by plane |
| *in autobus* | by bus |
| *in bicicletta*, *in bici* | by bike |
| **a piedi** | on foot |

*Con* with the article is also correct and common: *Vado a Roma con il treno* (or *col treno*). *Con* is the one to use for a particular vehicle: *Vado con la macchina di Marco* (I'm going in Marco's car).

Watch **a piedi** (on foot): *in piedi* means "standing", which is something else.

## Where you're from

You know two ways to say where you're from: **essere di** and **venire da**.

> Sono di Milano. Vengo da Milano.
> I'm from Milan. I come from Milan.

With a country, *di* doesn't work: Italians use the nationality, *Sono inglese*. With **venire da**, the article joins, because countries have one:

> Vengo dall'Inghilterra. Vengo dagli Stati Uniti. Vengo dalla Francia.
> I come from England. I come from the United States. I come from France.

*Da* also means "from" in a more general sense, like coming home from somewhere: *Torno dal lavoro* (I'm coming back from work).

*Tornare* (to come back, to go back) is regular, like *parlare*: *torno, torni, torna, torniamo, tornate, tornano*.

## Di: of and 's

Italian has no *'s*. To say whose something is, put the owner after **di**:

> La macchina di Marco. Il cane della vicina.
> Marco's car. The neighbour's dog.

With *il, la, gli*... the joined forms appear: *il libro dello studente* (the student's book), *le chiavi dell'ufficio* (the office keys).

**Di** also links a container or amount to what's in it, as in English *of*: *un bicchiere di vino*, *una bottiglia d'acqua*. Before a vowel, *di* often becomes **d'**, but *di* is also correct: *una bottiglia di acqua*.

## Some: del, della, dei

*Di* joined with the article can also mean **some**. This is called the partitive.

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| Masculine | *del pane* (some bread), *dello zucchero*, *dell'olio* | *dei panini* (some sandwiches), *degli amici* |
| Feminine | *della pizza*, *dell'acqua* | *delle bottiglie* (some bottles) |

> Compro del pane e dei panini.
> I'm buying some bread and some sandwiches.

> Stasera vengono degli amici.
> Some friends are coming tonight.

*Olio* is oil: *dell'olio* (some oil).

The singular means "some, a bit of" with things you can't count (bread, water, sugar). The plural works like a plural of *un* / *una*: *un amico*, *degli amici*.

**Often you can leave it out.** Like English, Italian often says nothing at all: *Compro pane* and *Compro del pane* are both correct, as are *Ci sono libri sul tavolo* and *Ci sono dei libri sul tavolo*. In questions both are common, and in negatives it's left out:

> C'è latte? No, non c'è latte.
> Is there any milk? No, there's no milk.

> Non ho soldi.
> I don't have any money.

The partitive is optional in most places where English says "some", so you don't have to use it to be correct. Use it where it sounds natural to you, and notice it when you hear it.

## Typical mistakes

| ✗ | ✓ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Abito in Roma. | Abito a Roma. | Cities take *a*. |
| Vado a Italia. | Vado in Italia. | Countries take *in*. |
| Vado in gli Stati Uniti. | Vado negli Stati Uniti. | Plural country names take the joined form. |
| Il libro è su il tavolo. | Il libro è sul tavolo. | *Su* + *il* is always *sul*. |
| Vado al medico. | Vado dal medico. | To someone's place: *da*. |
| Sono da Roma. | Sono di Roma. | *Essere di*, *venire da*. |
| Vengo da Francia. | Vengo dalla Francia. | Countries keep their article after *da*. |
| Vado a scuola in piedi. | Vado a scuola a piedi. | *In piedi* means standing. |
| Il cane di la vicina. | Il cane della vicina. | *Di* + *la* is *della*. |

## Summary

**Joined forms.** *Di, a, da, in, su* join with the article: *del, al, dal, nel, sul*, and so on through *dello, della, dei, degli, delle*. *Col* and *coi* are optional; *per* and *tra / fra* never join.

**Places.** *A* with cities, *in* with countries and regions (*negli Stati Uniti* for plural names). In town, learn each place with its preposition: *a casa, a scuola*; *in centro, in ufficio, in banca*; *al bar, al supermercato, al mare*. *Da* with a person: *dal medico, da Marco*. The same words work for being somewhere and for going there.

**Transport.** *In treno, in macchina, in aereo, in autobus, in bici*; *a piedi*.

**Origin.** *Sono di Roma*; *vengo da Roma*, *vengo dalla Francia*.

**Di and some.** *La macchina di Marco* for whose; *del pane, dei panini* for some, often left out, and left out in negatives.
