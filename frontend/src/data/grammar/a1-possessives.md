# Possessive adjectives

*My*, *your*, *his*, *her*: Italian has these too, with two differences from English. They take the article (*il mio libro*, literally "the my book"), and they agree with the **thing** you own, not with the owner. This unit covers both, the one exception for family, and *suo* for formal *Lei*.

## The forms

| | Masculine | Feminine | Masc. plural | Fem. plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| my | *mio* | *mia* | *miei* | *mie* |
| your (*tu*) | *tuo* | *tua* | *tuoi* | *tue* |
| his, her, your (*Lei*) | *suo* | *sua* | *suoi* | *sue* |
| our | *nostro* | *nostra* | *nostri* | *nostre* |
| your (*voi*) | *vostro* | *vostra* | *vostri* | *vostre* |
| their | *loro* | *loro* | *loro* | *loro* |

*Loro* never changes. The plurals *miei*, *tuoi* and *suoi* are the irregular ones; the rest work like ordinary adjectives in *-o*.

**They agree with the thing owned.** *Mio* or *mia* depends on the noun, not on who's speaking. A woman says *il mio libro*; a man says *la mia casa*.

> Il mio libro. La mia casa. I miei amici. Le mie chiavi.
> My book. My house. My friends. My keys.

This matters most with **suo**: it means both "his" and "her", and agrees with the noun. *La sua macchina* is "his car" or "her car", depending on who you're talking about:

> Marco ha una macchina nuova. La sua macchina è rossa.
> Marco has a new car. His car is red.

> Giulia ha un cane. Il suo cane si chiama Leo.
> Giulia has a dog. Her dog is called Leo.

If it isn't clear whose it is, say the name with *di*: *la macchina di Marco*.

## The article

In Italian the possessive usually comes with the article: **il mio libro**, **la tua casa**, **i nostri amici**. Leaving it out, as in English, is a typical mistake.

> La nostra casa è piccola. Dove sono le tue chiavi?
> Our house is small. Where are your keys?

With *un*, the possessive means "of mine", "of yours":

> Un mio amico abita a Roma.
> A friend of mine lives in Rome.

A few places leave the article out and put the possessive after the noun. The common one is **a casa mia** (at my place), *a casa tua*, *a casa sua*:

> Stasera ceniamo a casa mia.
> Tonight we're having dinner at my place.

### Family: no article

With a **single family member**, the article goes:

> Mio fratello abita a Milano. Mia sorella è medico.
> My brother lives in Milan. My sister is a doctor.

This works with *padre* (father), *madre* (mother), *fratello*, *sorella*, *figlio*, *figlia*, *marito* (husband) and *moglie* (wife). The article comes back:

- in the **plural**: *i miei fratelli*, *i miei genitori*, *le mie sorelle*
- with **loro**: *la loro madre*, *il loro figlio*
- when the family word is **changed or described**: *il mio fratellino* (my little brother), *la mia sorella piccola* (my younger sister)

With **mamma** and **papà**, the more affectionate words, the article is common: *la mia mamma*, *il mio papà*. You'll also hear *mia mamma*.

> Mio padre e mia madre sono di Napoli. I miei genitori sono di Napoli.
> My father and my mother are from Naples. My parents are from Naples.

## Suo for Lei

**Suo** also means "your" when you're speaking to someone as *Lei*. It's the same word as "his" and "her", just as *Lei* is the same word as *lei* (she):

> Signora, è Sua questa borsa?
> Madam, is this your bag?

> Come si chiama Suo figlio?
> What's your son called?

The capital *S* is a courtesy some people use in formal writing, like the capital in *Lei*. In speech there's no difference.

## On their own: il mio, il tuo

Possessives can stand on their own, for "mine", "yours". Then they always have the article, even for family:

> Questo è il tuo caffè? No, il mio è lì.
> Is this your coffee? No, mine is over there.

> Mio fratello abita a Roma. E il tuo?
> My brother lives in Rome. And yours?

After *essere*, the article is often left out: *È tuo questo libro?* (Is this book yours?) *Sì, è mio.*

## Typical mistakes

| ✗ | ✓ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Mio libro è qui. | Il mio libro è qui. | Possessives usually take the article. |
| la mia madre | mia madre | A single family member: no article. |
| miei genitori | i miei genitori | Plural family: the article comes back. |
| il suo macchina (Marco's) | la sua macchina | *Suo* agrees with the car, not with Marco. |

## Summary

**Forms.** *Mio, tuo, suo, nostro, vostro* have four forms each (*mio, mia, miei, mie*); *loro* never changes.

**Agreement.** With the thing owned: *la sua macchina* is his car or her car. Use *di* + a name when it isn't clear.

**The article.** Usually there: *il mio libro*, *un mio amico* (a friend of mine). Not with a single family member (*mio padre, mia sorella*), but back with plurals (*i miei genitori*), with *loro* (*la loro madre*) and with a described family word (*il mio fratellino*). *La mia mamma* is common. *A casa mia* has no article.

**Formal.** *Suo* is also "your" for *Lei*.

**Mine, yours.** *Il mio, la tua*, always with the article on their own: *E il tuo?*
