# Subject pronouns & essere

*Essere* (to be) is the most important verb in Italian. You need it to say who you are, where you're from, what you do and what something is like. This unit covers essere in the present tense and the subject pronouns that go with it: *I*, *you*, *he*, *she* and the rest, including the three different ways Italian says "you".

## Subject pronouns

| | Singular | | Plural | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1st person | **io** | I | **noi** | we |
| 2nd person | **tu** | you (informal) | **voi** | you (plural) |
| 3rd person | **lui** / **lei** | he / she | **loro** | they |
| Formal | **Lei** | you (formal) | | |

- *Io* is written in lower case, unlike English "I". It only takes a capital at the start of a sentence.
- *Loro* covers any group: men, women or mixed.
- Italian has no everyday subject pronoun for "it". The verb is used on its own: *È grande.* (It's big.)
- In older or very formal writing you will meet **egli** (he), **ella** (she), **essi** and **esse** (they). Recognise them, but use *lui*, *lei* and *loro*: they are standard in speech and in writing.

### Tu, Lei and voi

You've already met the formal and informal phrases (*come stai? / come sta?*). Here are the pronouns behind them. Italian has three ways to say "you".

- **tu** for one person you know well: friends, family, children, and people your age in informal settings.
- **Lei** for one person in a formal or service setting, someone clearly older than you, or someone you owe respect to: a shop assistant, a doctor, a teacher, an older stranger.
- **voi** for two or more people, formal or informal.

*Lei* takes the same verb form as *lui* and *lei* (he, she). So *Lei è* means "you are" when you're speaking to someone formally, and *lei è* means "she is". In speech they sound identical and context decides.

> Signora, Lei è di Roma?
> Madam, are you from Rome?

> Sara? Lei è di Roma.
> Sara? She's from Rome.

When *Lei* is addressed to a man, anything that agrees with it is masculine; to a woman, feminine.

> Signor Bianchi, Lei è italiano?
> Mr Bianchi, are you Italian?

> Signora Bianchi, Lei è italiana?
> Mrs Bianchi, are you Italian?

The capital L in *Lei* is a sign of respect and is optional. Many people write *lei* in both meanings. This course writes *Lei* so you can always tell them apart. If you write it yourself, pick one style and keep to it.

You may also hear **Loro** as a formal plural "you" (in hotels and very polite service). It is old-fashioned; today *voi* is used for any group.

In parts of southern Italy **voi** is still used as a formal singular "you". You don't need to use it, but don't be surprised to hear it.

### Dropping the pronoun

The verb ending already shows who is doing the action, so Italian normally leaves the subject pronoun out.

> Sono Marco.
> I'm Marco.

> Siamo di Milano.
> We're from Milan.

Saying *Io sono di Milano* in a neutral sentence is not wrong, but it usually sounds emphatic, as if you're insisting on *I*. (In introductions, *Io sono Marco* is common too.) Use the pronoun when:

1. **You contrast two people.**
   > Io sono di Roma, lui è di Napoli.
   > I'm from Rome, he's from Naples.
2. **You stress who it is.**
   > Pago io.
   > I'll pay. (Me, not you.)
3. **The pronoun stands alone or after *anche* (also).**
   > Chi è di Milano? Io.
   > Who's from Milan? Me.

   > Sono stanco. Anch'io.
   > I'm tired. Me too.

   *Anch'io* and *anche io* are both correct.
4. **The verb alone is unclear.** *È di Roma* could be "he", "she" or formal "you" is from Rome. If context doesn't make it clear, add *lui*, *lei* or *Lei*.

## Essere: to be

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| io | **sono** | I am |
| tu | **sei** | you are |
| lui / lei / Lei | **è** | he / she is, you (formal) are |
| noi | **siamo** | we are |
| voi | **siete** | you (plural) are |
| loro | **sono** | they are |

*Essere* is irregular: its forms have to be learned by heart.

- **Sono** is both "I am" and "they are". Context, or a pronoun, tells them apart.
- **È** (is) has an accent. Without it, **e** means "and": *Marco è alto e simpatico.* (Marco is tall and nice.)
- At the start of a sentence, the capital is **È**. You will often see *E'* with an apostrophe, typed on keyboards without an È key, but *È* is the correct form.
- Before *è*, *dove* (where) and *come* (how, what ... like) drop their final *e*: *Dov'è?* (Where is it?), *Com'è?* (What's it like?). Before other words they stay whole: *Dove abiti?*

### What essere is used for

**Identity and names.**
> Sono Giulia. Questo è Luca.
> I'm Giulia. This is Luca.

> Chi è? È il professore.
> Who is it? It's the teacher.

**Where someone is from: essere di + city.**
> Di dove sei? Sono di Torino.
> Where are you from? I'm from Turin.

*Di* goes with a city or town. For a country, give your nationality instead: *Sono italiano*, *Sono inglese*. (You'll learn how to name the country itself with the prepositions.)

English "from" makes *da* a tempting guess, but *Sono da Londra* is wrong for where you're from. *Da* does mean "from" after verbs of movement (*vengo da Londra*, I come from London), which comes with the present tense.

**Nationality.**
> Sono americana.
> I'm American. (a woman speaking)

> Siamo tedeschi.
> We're German.

Most nationality adjectives change their ending to agree with the person (*italiano*, *italiana*, *italiani*, *italiane*): a man says *Sono italiano*, a woman says *Sono italiana*. Nationalities ending in **-e** are the same for both: *Sono inglese*, *Sono francese*. The unit on adjectives covers this properly.

**Profession.**
> Sono studente.
> I'm a student.

> Lei è medico?
> Are you a doctor?

With a profession on its own, Italian usually leaves out the article: *Sono insegnante* is more common than *Sono un insegnante*, though both are correct. Many job words also have a feminine form, so a woman says *Sono studentessa*; the unit on nouns covers these. When there's an adjective, the article is needed: *È un bravo insegnante.* (He's a good teacher.)

**Description.**
> La casa è grande.
> The house is big.

> Sei stanca?
> Are you tired? (to a woman)

**Location.**
> Sono a casa.
> I'm at home.

> Dov'è la stazione?
> Where's the station?

The small words *a*, *in* and *di* are covered in the unit on prepositions.

**"There is" and "there are"** use essere too: *C'è un bar* (there's a bar), *Ci sono due bar* (there are two bars). They're covered in the unit on nouns and articles.

### Essere and stare

Italian has a second verb that English also translates as "to be": **stare**. In standard Italian the split is clear for one case you need now: how you are (well, not well) uses *stare*.

> Come stai? Sto bene, grazie.
> How are you? I'm well, thanks.

*Sono bene* is wrong. Learn *sto bene* and *sto male* as set phrases for now; *stare* gets its full forms in the unit on irregular verbs.

*Stare* also means **to stay**, and you'll meet it in fixed phrases like *stare attento* (to be careful). But the line between the two verbs isn't the same everywhere. In much of central and southern Italy, people use *stare* where the standard uses *essere*, especially for where someone is: *Sto a Napoli* (I'm in Naples), *Dove stai?* (Where are you?). You'll hear this, and it's normal in those regions. In standard Italian, use **essere** for who and where you are (*Sono a casa*) and **stare** for how you are (*Sto bene*).

## Questions and negatives

A question has the same words as a statement. In speech, your voice rises at the end; in writing, you add a question mark.

> Sei italiano.
> You're Italian.

> Sei italiano?
> Are you Italian?

To make a sentence negative, put **non** directly before the verb.

> Non sono di Roma.
> I'm not from Rome.

> Marco non è a casa.
> Marco isn't at home.

> Non siamo studenti.
> We aren't students.

A short answer can be just *sì* or *no*, and "no" before a negative sentence is common: *Sei stanco? No, non sono stanco.* (Are you tired? No, I'm not tired.) Questions and negatives are covered in full in their own unit.

## Typical mistakes

| ✗ | ✓ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sono da Londra. | Sono di Londra. | Where you're from is *essere di* + city. |
| Sono di Italia. | Sono italiano / italiana. | *Di* goes with a city. For a country, use your nationality. |
| Signora, sei di Roma? | Signora, è di Roma? | Formal *Lei* takes the 3rd-person verb, *è*. |
| Sono americano. (a woman) | Sono americana. | Nationalities in *-o* change for a woman. (Those in *-e*, like *inglese*, don't.) |
| Lui e alto. | Lui è alto. | *è* (is) needs its accent; *e* means "and". |
| Sono bene. | Sto bene. | "How you are" uses stare. |

## Summary

| | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| io | I | **sono** | I am |
| tu | you (informal) | **sei** | you are |
| lui / lei | he / she | **è** | he / she is |
| Lei | you (formal) | **è** | you are |
| noi | we | **siamo** | we are |
| voi | you (plural) | **siete** | you are |
| loro | they | **sono** | they are |

Use **tu** with one person you know well, **Lei** (with the *è* form) with one person formally, and **voi** with any group. Leave the pronoun out unless you're contrasting, stressing, or using it on its own (*Anch'io*). Essere says who someone is, where they're from (*di* + city), their nationality and job, what they're like and where they are; how they *are* uses *stare* (*Sto bene*). For a question, raise your voice; for a negative, put **non** before the verb.
